{\footnotesize\color{Muted}Kimi K3, training + evaluation}

\column{.62\textwidth}
{\small
\begin{ticks}
\item \textbf{pause} the sandbox while the model thinks, up to 98\% of its life, using no memory or CPU
\item \textbf{fork} from the exact state while the original keeps running, ``for reward judging without side effects''
\item \textbf{snapshot} at regular intervals, for error recovery
\item \textbf{partial rollout}: optimise once a fraction $\lambda$ of trajectories (attempts) completes, ``to mitigate the long-tail latency''
\end{ticks}\par}
\end{columns}
\vspace{.15cm}
{\small DeepSeek-V4's DSec runs hundreds of thousands of concurrent sandboxes in one cluster.\par}
\vspace{.05cm}
{\footnotesize\color{Muted}Partial rollouts follow Dean \& Barroso's ``good enough'' approach of not waiting for the slowest attempts.\par}
\claim{In training, a sandbox is the environment, a fork is a reset, and a verifier is the reward.}

\note{[1:00] Model training is the largest reported use of fork. Kimi K3 created fifty-one million sandboxes for training and evaluation. It pauses a sandbox while the model thinks, up to ninety-eight percent of its life. It forks one, in their words, for reward judging without side effects. It snapshots for recovery. The trainer stops waiting once a fraction lambda of trajectories, meaning attempts, finishes, to mitigate the long-tail latency. This matches Dean and Barroso's good-enough approach of not waiting for stragglers. DeepSeek-V4 runs hundreds of thousands of sandboxes per cluster.
